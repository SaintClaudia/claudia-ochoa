Case Study — 02 · Deep Dive

A closer look at the moments that matter.

From mobile navigation and visual discovery to Room Fit and the smaller interaction decisions throughout the experience, this deep dive explores how the strategy translates into a more useful, confidence-building customer journey.

01 · Mobile Nav

Simplifying the mobile menu.

When everything is prioritized, nothing is. The mobile redesign creates a clearer hierarchy so customers can get to the products and information that matter faster.

The original menu lists every product category flat, one after another, each with its own description and chevron — eight-plus rows before you even reach account actions, with a floating chat tab overlapping the content the whole way down. The redesign collapses that into two categories, Furniture and Accessories, plus direct links to what customers actually need next: delivery expectations, a nearby store, support, and their account.

Lovesac.com — tapping "Sectionals"
Redesign — tapping "Furniture"

The pattern repeats one level deeper, and this is where visual language starts to matter. Tapping into a category on Lovesac.com just expands more text — same flat rows, same chevrons, no cue for what you're about to see. The redesign switches to photography at this tier: real product tiles a customer can recognize at a glance, instead of parsing category names like "Snugg" or "StealthTech" cold.

02 · Desktop Nav

The same principle, scaled up to the mega-menu.

On desktop, the flat-text pattern shows up again as multi-column dropdown menus — long lists of links with no visual cue for what's actually being sold. The redesign carries the same photo-first approach from mobile up to the full-width nav.

Lovesac.com — Sectionals menu
Redesign — Furniture menu

The original Sectionals menu spreads products across three columns of plain text links, with a single lifestyle photo off to the side captioned "Order Free Swatches" — though the photo itself shows a family on a couch, not a single swatch. Everything else stays unillustrated, so a shopper has to read every row. The redesign replaces the list with a photo grid: each product is its own recognizable tile, turning browsing into visual scanning instead of reading.

Lovesac.com — Accessories menu
Redesign — Accessories menu

Same problem, same fix, one level over: the Accessories menu goes from four columns of unillustrated text to a photo grid a shopper can scan in seconds. Applied consistently across every top-level category, the mega-menu stops being a list to read and becomes a shelf to browse.

The mega-menu redesign isn't just visual — it reflects a different underlying structure.

  • 1Two kinds of links, treated as one. The eight items in the original nav aren't actually the same category of thing — some are content navigation ("what can I browse?"), some are utility navigation ("what can I do, regardless of what I'm browsing?"). The original doesn't just flatten them together; it scatters the utility group across two different bars with two different treatments — Store Locator and Quote Lookup as text in the gray strip above, Account/Cart/Search as icons in the white row below. Same function, two systems.

This is a standard IA convention: separate primary (content) navigation from utility (task) navigation, and keep utility in one consistent system. Store Locator moves down into the icon row because it was never a browsing category to begin with; Quote Lookup moves inside Account because a saved quote is account-specific, not a standalone site-wide function.

  • 2Six product lines, one product type. Sectionals, Couches, Chairs, Sacs, StealthTech, and Accessories collapsing into a single Furniture menu isn't just fewer clicks — it reduces cognitive load on two fronts. Fewer top-level choices means faster decisions, since decision time increases with the number of options presented (Hick's Law); and grouping related items into fewer categories makes the structure easier to process and remember (Miller's Law) (Yablonski, 2018).

  • 3"What to Expect" isn't generic — it's a direct response. Cahill's complaint wasn't really about the couch; it was about not knowing what he was signing up for. Twenty-four boxes, hours of assembly, gloves recommended by other customers before he'd even opened the first box — none of that was set as an expectation anywhere before purchase. The backlash that followed showed this wasn't just his experience; commenters flooded in with their own versions of the same surprise.

    The original site has no equivalent category. Assembly, delivery, and trial details exist, but scattered — a "60-Day Home Trial" mention in the utility bar, warranty info buried in the footer, nothing that tells a first-time buyer what unboxing a Sactional actually involves. "What to Expect" as a dedicated third nav category is a direct structural answer to that gap: not a policy page, a pre-purchase category — the thing a shopper checks before the box shows up, not after they're upset about it.

The redesign's "What to Expect" page puts the real numbers in front of a shopper before they buy, not after hours into a build wondering what they signed up for.

03 · New Feature

Checking if it fits, before you buy.

Before someone commits to a Sactional configuration, they want to know one thing: will it fit? This feature answers that inline, without leaving the page.

Will it fit your room?

Share your room dimensions and we'll help you find setups that fit comfortably.

Will It Fit?

Room dimensions
Enter your room dimensions to see what fits.
See it come together
Add any furniture you want to keep. We'll factor it into the fit.
Entry point · tap Check Your Space to try it

Two taps from "I like this" to "I know it'll fit."

The entry point is a simple, low-commitment: a simple question and a single button, sitting right where a shopper is already. No app download, no account, no separate flow.

Opening it reveals a short form — room width and length, plus an optional section to add existing furniture so the layout accounts for what's already in the room. It's scoped to answer one question well, rather than becoming a full room-design tool.

  • 1Removes a real purchase blocker. Furniture is a high-consideration purchase; "will it fit" is the anxiety that stalls a cart the most.
  • 2Fills a real gap. Lovesac doesn't currently offer any way to check room fit online — shoppers are left to eyeball dimensions or ask in-store. Room Fit answers the question right where they're already shopping.
  • 3Start simple, build toward fit-aware shopping. The initial experience asks only for what's needed to answer the immediate fit question. A future version could use those dimensions to filter configurations, account for existing furniture, and visualize multiple layouts at true scale.
  • 4Built for agentic shopping, not just human browsing. Each Sactionals setup in the working prototype carries schema.org Product markup — name, price, and width/depth as structured properties — so an AI shopping agent or search crawler can reason about what fits a room straight from the page source, without executing any JavaScript. Same "agentic commerce" capability leadership named as a strategic priority in Section 03.
04 · Accessibility

Accessibility is the experience.

This is a live capture from the mobile site, not a mockup. Turning on "Accessibility Mode" overlays a second, misaligned promo banner on top of the first and leaves an autoplaying, sound-adjacent video sitting directly beneath it.

Mostly structural noise — "Image link, no label," a "Shop by Product carousel" region, eleven identical "Show Slide" buttons in a row — not what the page is actually trying to say.

Redesign — accessible by default
Hear it: My Redesign
Stopped · 0%

The redesign's own copy carries it — headline, trial terms, section titles read back almost exactly as written, giving the brand an actual voice instead of a list of unlabeled controls.

Press play on either card above to hear the announcement order a screen reader gives each version — synthesized live in your browser, not a recording. The Lovesac.com script is approximated from a markup review of the live site rather than a captured screen-reader session, and its promotional carousel copy can drift as the site updates; the redesign script matches this case study page's current markup.

Two problems are stacked together: first, accessibility is treated as an optional mode a visitor has to find and enable, rather than the default experience. Second, enabling it doesn't produce a cleaner page — it duplicates the promo banner text and leaves the video in place, requiring the visitor to decide whether to play it. An overlay intended to reduce cognitive load instead adds another visual layer to parse.

The redesign takes a different approach: accessibility is built into the default experience. Sufficient contrast, no autoplaying media, and one banner state create a simpler, more consistent experience for every visitor.

05 · SEO

SEO that works.

Scroll to the very bottom of the mobile site and the footer ends in an unbroken wall of trademarked product names — not a sitemap, not links, just plain visible text stacking every brand term the company owns.

Lovesac.com — keyword wall
Redesign — footer

This reads like a keyword-stuffing pattern from over fifteen years ago — the belief that visibly listing every trademarked term on the page would boost search rankings. Modern search engines have long since discounted (and can penalize) exactly this kind of content: dense, unstructured keyword lists with no navigational value to an actual visitor. It's also an accessibility cost with no SEO upside — a screen reader passes the legal links and copyright, then has to read through dozens of product names in the trademark list before it's done with the footer.

The fix isn't removing the trademark disclosure — that's a legitimate legal requirement. It's separating legal necessity from SEO strategy: move the list off the visible page and behind a single "Trademarks" link next to Privacy Policy and Terms — the same legal-row pattern the original site already uses, just extended by one link. The disclosure is still one tap away for anyone who needs it; it's no longer doing double duty as a keyword-stuffing block for search engines that stopped rewarding this years ago.

06 · Trust Signals

Proof, front and center.

The real site's trust content — warranty, trial period, shipping, delivery — doesn't live on the homepage at all. It's on the Sactionals product page, collapsed behind four separate accordions a visitor has to find and tap open one at a time.

Lovesac.com — Sactionals PDP
Redesign — homepage
60-Day Trial — try it at home, then decide
Machine Washable — easy to remove, easy to wash
Forever Warranty — your frame covered for life
Recycled Fill — made from reclaimed plastic bottles

Reaching this information requires navigating to a specific product page and scrolling past a lifestyle image of the founder and his family. A shopper browsing the homepage never encounters it. Even there, the key details remain collapsed behind a plus icon: "Guaranteed For Life," "60-Day Home Trial," "Free Shipping and Returns," and "Room of Choice Delivery."

The redesign brings the trust signals forward, placing the key benefits in a scannable row directly beneath the homepage hero. The specific value is visible at a glance rather than hidden behind an interaction. A shopper shouldn't have to dig for reasons to trust the brand—they should encounter them early in the journey.

  • 1Interaction cost. The original hides six of its ten testimonials behind a single small arrow — a carousel. Carousels have a well-documented problem in HCI: low discoverability and low interaction rates, because anything not immediately visible gets treated as "not there." The redesign shows all three reviews at once, zero clicks required — it front-loads the proof instead of gating it behind an action.
  • 2Trust mechanism trade-off. The original leans on real customer room photos — authentic, but inconsistent lighting and color across four different homes creates visual noise, and it costs more to load and scan. The redesign drops the photos entirely and substitutes a "Verified Buyer" checkmark instead — a different kind of trust signal (platform-verified vs. visually authentic), consistent card heights, and faster to scan.
  • 3Signifier placement. The original states a 4.3-star aggregate once, above the section — then none of the four testimonial cards carry a rating of their own, so nothing below actually substantiates the number. The redesign puts a 5-star rating directly on every card, so each testimonial supports the claim it's part of, instead of asking the visitor to take the aggregate on faith.
08 · Financing

A financing offer worth seeing.

On the homepage, the financing offer appears as a simple text link between the "Shop by Product" section and an image carousel. There's little visual distinction to signal that it's a meaningful purchase consideration—especially for a high-ticket product.

Lovesac.com — promo stack
Redesign — Lovesac Credit Card

On the original homepage, the offer is easy to overlook. It appears as a text link with no supporting imagery, headline, or explanation of the financing terms until the customer follows the link. For a $1,000+ purchase, that makes a potentially useful path to purchase feel like secondary promotional content.

The redesign gives financing a dedicated band with a clear headline, card imagery, the specific terms—24 months, subject to credit approval—and a single "Apply now" action. The offer itself hasn't changed; the experience gives it the context and visibility it deserves.

The card itself got a second look, too. The Synchrony-issued card carries Lovesac's actual brand mark, but the design reads closer to a generic store card than the rest of the brand experience. A set of design options explores what the card could look like with more of Lovesac's own visual identity. That same approach could extend to gift cards, creating a more cohesive branded experience.

Extending the system The same idea applies naturally to gift cards — another card-shaped touchpoint that could carry more of Lovesac's visual identity. Rather than a one-off redesign, this becomes a small design system: a set of credit card and gift card faces built from the same visual language.

There's a bigger gap here than card design. Lovesac currently offers a single branded eGift card and no physical gift card to purchase in-store. Occasion-specific options could make digital gifting feel more personal while extending that same choice to the in-store experience. More than 50% of people still prefer the tangible nature of physical gift cards for personal gifting (Mullen, 2022).

That makes the opportunity commercial, not just visual. Gift cards can generate upfront cash flow, incremental spend beyond the card value, and revenue from balances that go unredeemed. Globally, the gift card market is valued at approximately $1.49 trillion in 2026 and is projected to surpass $2.22 trillion by 2034 (Fortune Business Insights, 2026).
