The Business Case

What started with a founder and CEO publicly poking fun at a customer became an opportunity to rethink the experience, the product, and the business around it.

August 26, 2026 · 6 min read

01 · The Problem

The product is flexible. The experience wasn't.

Lovesac sells a highly configurable product, but its digital experience made that complexity harder to navigate, understand, and act on. The opportunity was to bring the same clarity, flexibility, and confidence to the customer journey.

  • 1The value proposition isn't clear enough. Customers encounter promotions and product terminology before they understand what makes Lovesac different or why the product is worth the investment.
  • 2The experience creates unnecessary complexity. Navigation, product choices, and content make a highly configurable product feel harder to understand rather than easier.
  • 3The experience doesn't build enough purchase confidence. Important questions — will it fit? How does it work? What happens after I buy? Can I live with it? — aren't answered at the moments customers need them.
  • 4The digital experience isn't delivering on the brand promise. A company selling flexibility and longevity presents an experience that can feel cluttered, promotional, and reactive rather than considered and premium.
02 · The Approach

Turn complexity into confidence.

The strategy was simple: remove friction from the customer journey without removing the flexibility that makes the product different. That meant simplifying discovery, bringing critical purchase questions into the experience, and making accessibility part of the foundation rather than an optional layer.

  • 1Simplify discovery. Visual navigation, clearer hierarchy, fewer competing paths.
  • 2Make the product easier to understand. Stronger value communication and more useful product information.
  • 3Build purchase confidence. Room Fit, clearer expectations, trial and warranty information.
  • 4Make accessibility foundational. Contrast, focus states, heading structure, skip navigation, no autoplay.
03 · The Research

Public reviews back some of it up.

A pass through Reddit threads, BBB complaints, and review sites — no internal analytics, no usability testing.

Assembly difficulty, support access, warranty ambiguity, and StealthTech setup friction are real, recurring complaints — and the redesign was already responding to several of these before this research was formalized. The research reinforced the need to make setup expectations clearer, improve access to support, and clarify the distinction between frame and cushion warranty coverage.

If this shipped, here's what I'd track to know whether it actually worked:

1
Conversion rate — PDP → cart

The primary measure of the redesign's purchase-path changes: are fewer decision points helping more customers move forward?

2
Room Fit engagement

The percentage of product-page visitors who use Room Fit before adding to cart, showing whether customers find the tool useful at the point of decision.

3
Return rate — Room Fit users vs. non-users

The stronger test of the tool's value: whether greater confidence in how a Sactional will fit translates into fewer returns.

4
Time to purchase

Time from landing to checkout, compared with the current site benchmark, to see whether a clearer path reduces friction without rushing the customer through decisions.

05 · The Business Value

Designing for value, not just experience.

Each recommendation solves a specific customer or operational problem, but the opportunity extends beyond the experience itself.

Together, they create multiple paths to business value — increasing conversion and revenue, protecting margin, reducing the cost to serve, strengthening retention, and opening new channels for growth. The connection to shareholder value isn't any single feature; it's the cumulative effect of making the business easier to buy from, easier to operate, and better positioned to grow.

06 · The Outcome

What real compensation looks like.

Derick Cahill was offered a pair of floral gardening gloves, size small. This case study is what happens when you take the actual problem seriously: set better expectations, listen to what customers are actually saying, and run a full holistic evaluation.

The gloves were a joke. The redesign isn't.

Business school teaches you that a mission statement isn't a wall plaque — it's the thing every decision is supposed to answer to. Lovesac's runs on the word "love." The apology that followed the gloves was not an apology. Nor is it one of a true leader.

This gap is what the redesign actually answers — unhappy customers, a dated brand, an imperfect product, and a mission statement that never makes it past the investor page. I didn't just point at what's broken. I rebuilt it, the way I'd run it.

The actual gloves.

After thoughtful consideration, I've decided to withdraw my name from consideration as Lovesac's next CEO.
