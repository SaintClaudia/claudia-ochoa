Case Study 04 · Content Strategy

Say it like you mean it.

How emotional intelligence shaped the voice and content across the redesign, from answering practical questions to building a stronger connection with customers.

August 27, 2026 · 10 min read

The Thesis

Shaping brands with emotional intelligence means understanding what a customer needs in a given moment — and designing for it.

This is where emotional intelligence becomes the design. A brand voice isn't just how it sounds. It's how it makes the customer feel.

01 · Emotional Intelligence

First impressions.

The first encounter has a job to do: make the brand clear, give the customer a reason to stay, and make the next step obvious.

Lovesac.com — original hero
Redesign — new hero

The original design is visually busy — the over-the-top animation adds to the sensory overload. On top of that, it leads with "THE ONLY COUCH THAT CAN BE ANY COUCH," set entirely in caps.

In UX, all-caps is typically reserved for short, secondary text — eyebrows, tags, labels — not a headline someone needs to read at a glance.

That headline assumes the customer already knows the brand. It gives no regard to entry point — someone landing there for the first time, whether from a Google search or a showroom visit, likely doesn't have the "any couch" concept down yet. So the headline lands as a slogan instead of a message.

It also gives no call to action. The hero is clickable, but nothing tells you where it goes. Given the promo banners above and below it, the natural assumption is that clicking leads to the sale. Instead, it takes you to the Sactionals product page, where everything is on sale.

The redesign's "One couch. Built for life." doesn't require prior brand knowledge — and it doesn't leave the claim to hang there by itself. It's immediately followed by "Rearrange, re-cover, and grow — from a studio apartment to a family room, without having to buy another couch again." That line gives the headline context, followed by a clear call to action: "Shop now." No guessing where the click leads — the voice says what it means and tells you what happens next.

02 · Brand Voice

Words matter.

The redesign replaces hard-sell claims with familiar phrases that create meaning without having to spell everything out.

The original site leans on claims: the only, the world's most, guaranteed for life. With promotional claims doing most of the work, the voice leaves little room for the customer to connect with the brand on a human level. It also creates a credibility risk: the bigger the claim, the more the brand has to prove.

The redesign takes a different approach: warm, idiomatic, and slightly wry.

"Loved in real homes." Rather than simply labeling a section "Reviews," the phrase borrows the language of lived-in spaces and implies authenticity without having to announce it.

"Make yourself comfortable." A familiar phrase a host might say to a guest also happens to describe exactly what the product promises. As the headline for the financing offer, it turns a transactional message into something warmer and more inviting, while the supporting copy delivers the practical details.

"Labor of love." Applied to assembly, the phrase transforms what could feel like a chore into something warmer and more human. It acknowledges the work without making the experience feel like an instruction manual.

The throughline is found language rather than invented language. The redesign takes phrases people already understand and gives them a new context, allowing the meaning to do some of the persuasive work.

That distinction matters. The original voice tells customers what to believe. The redesign gives them something they already recognize — and lets them make the connection themselves.

There is also a deliberate connection to the brand itself. Lovesac already has love at the center of its name and mission. The language builds on that existing idea rather than layering a personality onto the brand from the outside.

03 · Product Storytelling

Make it easier to imagine.

Instead of simply saying "the only couch that can be any couch," the redesign shows what that looks like across the different chapters of a customer's life.

The original experience immediately asks the customer to shop by the company's product taxonomy:

On its own, there's nothing wrong with organizing a site around products. The problem is what that asks the customer to do before they've had a reason to care. They have to understand the product categories first, then figure out which one fits their life.

The language also keeps the customer at a distance. Sactionals, StealthTech, Snugg are names that make sense once you know the Lovesac ecosystem. For someone encountering the brand for the first time, they're product names — not reasons to connect.

The redesign starts from the other direction.

Stage 01
First apartment.
Stage 02
Moving in together.
Stage 03
Kids, dogs, chaos.
Stage 04
The house you'll stay in.

These are situations people already understand. They don't need an explanation, and they don't require prior knowledge of the product line.

The product becomes easier to understand because the customer can see where they fit into the story. A loveseat and ottoman make sense in a first apartment. Washable covers make sense when there are kids and dogs. A sectional that can split into two rooms makes sense when a home — and life — gets bigger.

The content isn't just describing what the product can do. It's giving the customer a reason to imagine what it could do for them.

Why This Is Strategy

Content strategy isn't about giving every page the same personality. It's about understanding what the content needs to do in each moment, then choosing the right language to do it.

Some moments need warmth. Some need personality. Some just need a clear answer. The strategy is knowing the difference—and making those choices deliberately.

04 · Product Discovery

Give people — and agents — something to recognize.

The product grid is where the customer's interest has to become a decision. This is where naming, context, and useful signals can make the difference between recognizing a product and having to decode one — and increasingly, that same clear signal is what lets a shopping agent recognize the product too, not just a person.

Lovesac.com — product grid
Redesign — product grid

The original homepage includes a series of names over the hero imagery: Anytime Conversations, Movie Marathons, Pet-Friendly Relaxing, Power Trios, Cross Talk, Capital L Lounging, Lefty Loungers, and Island Mentality.

It's worth noting, but the naming stops there.

Once the customer moves into the actual shopping experience, those names disappear. The sectional grid and product pages revert to configurations such as 4 Seats + 5 Sides or 6 Seats (1 Deep) + 8 Sides (1 Deep). The playful language from the homepage doesn't carry into the place where the customer actually has to choose a product.

There is also the "Customer Favorites" badge. Nearly every product in the default view carries it. When a signal appears across nearly every product, it stops helping the customer distinguish one product from another. It also loses trust.

The redesign takes a different approach.

Instead of making the customer decode a configuration, each setup is given a name that describes the experience it is designed for:

The Weekend Lounger
Fits 4 · Great for small living rooms
The Movie Night Sectional
Fits 6 · Reclining seats included
The Game Day U-Shape
Fits 8 · Room for the whole crew
The Family Room Corner
Fits 6 · 1-deep chaise

The name creates recognition. The supporting detail provides the facts.

The result is a product grid that gives customers something to understand before asking them to figure out what the numbers mean.

The same principle carries into the smaller details: when information is easier to recognize, it becomes easier to use.

Lovesac describes color in the product card: "Buy Now in Charcoal Grey Corded Velvet." The customer has to read the name and translate it into a visual impression. The redesign shows the color instead, using a small swatch beneath each product so recognition happens at a glance rather than through a sentence.

Pricing has the same problem, only visually instead of verbally. Every product card carries a struck-through original price and a red sale price. When everything is marked down, the treatment stops functioning as a signal. Red directs attention everywhere, which ultimately means it directs attention nowhere.

The redesign keeps pricing in plain type by default. That creates a calmer browsing experience — and preserves emphasis for the moments when something actually needs to stand out.

05 · Support Experience

Help first. Ask later.

When someone arrives looking for support, they're often looking for reassurance before they're ready to fill out a form. The redesign puts that reassurance first — and makes getting to a real person the easiest path.

On mobile, the current Contact Us page opens with "Send Us a Note" and immediately asks for required information: Name, Email, Phone, Order #. Above the form sits a warranty and damages disclaimer.

For someone arriving from search, this is likely to be their first interaction with the page. Instead of answering "Can someone help me?" the experience starts by asking the customer to do something.

The redesign changes the sequence:

  • 1We're here to help. A plain-language introduction acknowledges the customer before asking for anything.
  • 2Talk to a real person. Call — and ideally Chat/Text — are surfaced immediately, without making the customer work through the page to find them.
  • 3One path to help. Warranty, damage, order questions, and everything else don't need to create separate emotional entry points. The customer needs help; the experience should start there.

The result is simple: the customer gets an answer before they're asked to give one.

06 · Visual Storytelling

Make the product feel at home.

The existing photography was fixed: clean product cutouts on white. Without a reshoot, the redesign uses the interface itself to create more warmth and context, giving the product a stronger sense of place while working with the photography already available.

The original

The product photography is clean and consistent, but the white background leaves each piece visually isolated. On a product grid, the effect is closer to a catalog: product, price, information, repeat.

Lifestyle photography solves this differently. A sofa in an actual room immediately gives the customer context—scale, surroundings, light, texture, and a sense of how the product might feel in their own home.

That wasn't a lever available to this redesign.

The redesign

Instead, the page itself becomes part of the visual story. The stark white background shifts to a warm, oatmeal-toned neutral that sits closer to the colors and materials of the products.

The change is subtle, but it changes the relationship between the product and the page. The furniture feels more grounded and connected to the environment it's ultimately meant to live in, rather than floating against a blank canvas.

There is also a practical benefit to moving away from pure white. On an emissive screen, a large white surface produces more light than a darker or warmer neutral. Reducing that visual intensity makes the page feel less stark, particularly when the interface occupies most of the screen.

Lovesac.com — product grid
Redesign — product grid

This is not an attempt to recreate lifestyle photography with a background color. It can't provide the depth, dimensionality, or emotional pull of a real room.

It's a lighter-weight solution to the same underlying problem: how do you make a product feel at home before the customer has brought it home?

Here, the answer was to change the environment around the product—not the product photography itself.
