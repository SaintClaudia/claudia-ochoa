Case Study — 04 · Voice & Content Strategy

Say it like you mean it.

How emotional intelligence shaped the voice and content across the redesign, from answering practical questions to building a stronger connection with customers.

August 27, 2026 · 7 min read

The Thesis

Shaping brands with emotional intelligence means understanding what a customer needs in a given moment — and designing for it.

This is where emotional intelligence becomes the design. A brand voice isn't just how it sounds. It's how it makes the customer feel.

01 · Emotional Intelligence

First impressions.

The first encounter has a job to do: make the brand clear, give the customer a reason to stay, and make the next step obvious.

Lovesac.com — original hero
Redesign — new hero

The original design is visually busy — the over-the-top animation adds to the sensory overload. On top of that, it leads with "THE ONLY COUCH THAT CAN BE ANY COUCH," set entirely in caps.

In UX, all-caps is typically reserved for short, secondary text — eyebrows, tags, labels — not a headline someone needs to read at a glance.

That headline assumes the customer already knows the brand. It gives no regard to entry point — someone landing there for the first time, whether from a Google search or a showroom visit, likely doesn't have the "any couch" concept down yet. So the headline lands as a slogan instead of a message.

It also gives no call to action. The hero is clickable, but nothing tells you where it goes. Given the promo banners above and below it, the natural assumption is that clicking leads to the sale. Instead, it takes you to the Sactionals product page.

The redesign's "One couch. Built for life." doesn't require prior brand knowledge — and it doesn't leave the claim to hang there by itself. It's immediately followed by "Rearrange, re-cover, and grow — from a studio apartment to a family room, without having to buy another couch again." That line gives the headline context, followed by a clear call to action: "Shop now." No guessing where the click leads — the voice says what it means and tells you what happens next.

02 · Brand Voice

Words matter.

The redesign replaces hard-sell claims with familiar phrases that create meaning without having to spell everything out.

The original site leans on claims: the only, the world's most, guaranteed for life. With promotional claims doing most of the work, the voice leaves little room for the customer to connect with the brand on a human level. It also creates a credibility risk: the bigger the claim, the more the brand has to prove.

The redesign takes a different approach: warm, idiomatic, and slightly wry.

"Loved in real homes." Rather than simply labeling a section "Reviews," the phrase borrows the language of lived-in spaces and implies authenticity without having to announce it.

"Make yourself comfortable." A familiar phrase a host might say to a guest also happens to describe exactly what the product promises. As the headline for the financing offer, it turns a transactional message into something warmer and more inviting, while the supporting copy delivers the practical details.

"Labor of love." Applied to assembly, the phrase transforms what could feel like a chore into something warmer and more human. It acknowledges the work without making the experience feel like an instruction manual.

The throughline is found language rather than invented language. The redesign takes phrases people already understand and gives them a new context, allowing the meaning to do some of the persuasive work.

That distinction matters. The original voice tells customers what to believe. The redesign gives them something they already recognize — and lets them make the connection themselves.

There is also a deliberate connection to the brand itself. Lovesac already has love at the center of its name and mission. The language builds on that existing idea rather than layering a personality onto the brand from the outside.

03 · Product Storytelling

Make it easier to imagine.

Instead of simply saying "the only couch that can be any couch," the redesign shows what that looks like across the different chapters of a customer's life.

The original experience immediately asks the customer to shop by the company's product taxonomy:

Shop by Product
  • Our Recommended Picks
  • 20% Off Select Sactionals
  • 40% Off StealthTech Systems
  • 40% Off Snugg

On its own, there's nothing wrong with organizing a site around products. The problem is what that asks the customer to do before they've had a reason to care. They have to understand the product categories first, then figure out which one fits their life.

The language also keeps the customer at a distance. Sactionals, StealthTech, Snugg are names that make sense once you know the Lovesac ecosystem. For someone encountering the brand for the first time, they're product names — not reasons to connect.

The redesign starts from the other direction.

Stage 01

First apartment.

Stage 02

Moving in together.

Stage 03

Kids, dogs, chaos.

Stage 04

The house you'll stay in.

These are situations people already understand. They don't need an explanation, and they don't require prior knowledge of the product line.

The product becomes easier to understand because the customer can see where they fit into the story. A loveseat and ottoman make sense in a first apartment. Washable covers make sense when there are kids and dogs. A sectional that can split into two rooms makes sense when a home — and life — gets bigger.

The content isn't just describing what the product can do. It's giving the customer a reason to imagine what it could do for them.

Why This Is Strategy

A content strategy isn't "write everything warmly" or "write everything efficiently" — it's knowing, screen by screen, which job the copy is doing, and having the discipline to switch registers when the job changes. Every choice in this section — the idiom-borrowed voice, the scenario naming, the plain utility copy, even the honest gaps I haven't solved yet — comes from applying that one question consistently: what does this customer need to feel right now, and does this line of copy give them that, or get in the way of it?

That discipline is what "shaping brands with emotional intelligence" actually means in practice.

Scope

This case study covers a four-page concept, not a full site rebuild — homepage, sectional grid, support/contact, and the "what to expect" process page. The goal wasn't to polish one splashy hero; it was to prove the thinking holds up everywhere it touches, including the least glamorous screens. It's a starting point, and some pieces — like material-based color categorization — are flagged here as future research rather than finished answers.
