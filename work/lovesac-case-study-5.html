Case Study — 04 · Voice & Content Strategy

Say it like you mean it.

How emotional intelligence shaped the voice and content across the redesign, from answering practical questions to building a stronger connection with customers.

August 27, 2026 · 7 min read

The Thesis

Shaping brands with emotional intelligence means understanding what a customer needs in a given moment — and designing for it.

This is where emotional intelligence becomes the design. A brand voice isn't just how it sounds. It's how it makes the customer feel.

The Framework

Content vs. utility.

Every screen on a commerce site is doing one of two jobs.

Content moments are where a customer is still forming a feeling about the brand — the homepage, support pages, anywhere trust and reassurance actually move behavior. This is where voice should show up in full: warm, specific, human.

Utility moments are where the customer has already decided and just needs the interface to get out of the way — filters, calculators, room-fit tools, cart. Here, voice is a liability. Someone measuring their living room doesn't want a clever turn of phrase between them and the number they came for.

Most redesigns fail this test in one of two directions: they either flood the entire site with personality until it slows people down, or they stay so utilitarian everywhere that the brand never gets to say anything at all. The goal here was to apply voice only where it earns its keep, and stay out of the way everywhere else.

Applying the Framework

Across the site.

Homepage

"One couch. Built for life." leads with a promise, not a price or a spec — a deliberate departure from the live site's sale-first hero ("Labor Day Sale — up to 40% off"). The "Stage 01–04" section (First apartment → The house you'll stay in) turns the product into a life narrative instead of a spec sheet.

Idiom-borrowed voice

Rather than inventing new superlatives the way the original site does ("The World's Most Comfortable Seat," "Total Comfort"), the redesign borrows phrases people already have warm associations with and lets that familiarity carry the brand message:

  • 1"Make yourself comfortable." — doubles as a host's greeting and a literal product benefit.
  • 2"Labor of love." — dignifies assembly, the one step in the journey every competitor treats as a liability to minimize.
  • 3"Talk to a real person." — needs no idiom at all. It works because it implicitly contrasts with the chatbot-and-hold-music experience customers already expect by default.

Each phrase does real persuasive work by attaching the brand to a feeling the customer already trusts, rather than asking them to believe a new claim outright.

Naming, and where Lovesac's own version falls short

Lovesac's real homepage carries an animated banner of scenario-style labels layered over generic sofa renders: Anytime Conversations, Movie Marathons, Pet-Friendly Relaxing, Power Trios, Cross Talk, Capital L Lounging, Lefty Loungers, Island Mentality, The Archipelago, Face-to-Face Debriefs, Sunday Mavericks.

It's tempting to read this as "the same idea" the redesign builds on — but on closer inspection, it isn't. Most of these are internal wordplay, not customer-relatable naming: puns on configuration shape (Capital L Lounging = L-shape, Island Mentality / The Archipelago = a freestanding island configuration, Lefty Loungers = orientation) or vague mood phrases (Cross Talk, Face-to-Face Debriefs, Sunday Mavericks) that require the shopper to decode furniture-industry logic before the name means anything.

Even the names that should land — Movie Marathons is a genuinely relatable life moment — get diluted by the company they keep. Sitting in rotation next to Island Mentality and Cross Talk, a shopper's brain never gets the signal "these are moments I recognize"; the mix reads as one undifferentiated wash of cleverness, so even the one name that could resonate doesn't get the chance to.

And regardless of the copy, none of it survives past the hero: the moment a customer starts actually shopping, every listing drops to a raw configuration string — "6 Seats (1Deep) + 8 Sides (1Deep)" — that assumes the customer already understands modular-sofa logic.

The redesign takes a different approach, not a continuation of theirs: names built around a specific, recognizable use case, paired with a plain-language outcome instead of a spec string.

To be fair to Lovesac: the impulse toward personality-driven naming is there, and worth crediting. It's the execution — decode-it-yourself wordplay instead of recognizable life moments — that the redesign departs from.

Support

"Here's what happens." reassures before the customer has had to feel uncertain about the process. This is a register that would be wasted on a checkout button, but does real work on a page whose whole job is to lower anxiety before a big purchase.

Tools stay plain, on purpose

"Will it fit your room?" and the assembly-time estimator carry no idiom, no scenario name — just the fastest possible path to the number the customer came for. This isn't an oversight or a missed opportunity for more voice; it's the same discipline in the other direction. A customer mid-task doesn't want charm, they want an answer.

The redesign's pre-built browsing stage uses simple, single-word color names — Sand, Charcoal, Lagoon, Terracotta, Midnight, Forest, Espresso, Fog — built for fast scanning at a glance. This is worth being precise about: these names are original to the redesign, not pulled from Lovesac's site. Lovesac's actual fabric names are compound and descriptive — Sapphire Navy Corded Velvet, Venetian Taupe Corded Velvet, Amethyst Corded Velvet — which suit a full custom-builder view where the customer wants to know material and finish, but are slower to scan when someone just wants to filter by color at a glance.

An honest limitation Simplifying to single-word names works cleanly across a small pre-built set. Whether it holds up across a full custom palette (Lovesac's build tool offers 148+ options) is an open question, not a solved one. My instinct is that grouping by material family first, then color within that, would likely keep names simple even at scale — but that's a direction worth dedicated research, not a conclusion I've tested. I'd want to map the full material range before committing to it as the pattern.

A counter-example, in fairness

Lovesac's default sectional listing tags nearly every card "Customer Favorites" — which suggests the badge functions as a merchandising sort rather than a meaningful signal. It's a useful contrast to the naming work above: a badge applied that broadly stops being content (something true and specific) and becomes decorative noise. It's the opposite failure mode from over-using voice in a utility screen, but it comes from the same root cause — not asking what job a piece of copy is actually doing before deploying it everywhere.

A separate accessibility flag

Worth noting independently of the copy critique: the animated hero banner carrying those scenario names appears to use fast motion and flickering light effects. I wasn't able to fully assess the video directly, but if that read is accurate, it's a real photosensitivity concern — a legitimate accessibility gap sitting in the same part of the site as the naming issue, though a separate problem from it.

The Customer Journey

Where the register shifts, and why.

Laid end to end, the content-vs-utility split isn't just a rule applied screen by screen — it maps onto the actual path a customer walks, from first landing on the site to living with the couch. Each stage has a different emotional job, and the copy register shifts with it.

Two things stand out when the journey is mapped this way rather than page by page.

The register alternates, and that's intentional. A customer moves from Content (arrive, recognize, decide) to Utility (confirm fit, plan) and back to Content (reassure, own it). That's not inconsistency — it's the copy tracking the customer's actual state of mind. They're open to brand voice when they're still forming a feeling about the product, and they want it out of the way the moment they're heads-down on a practical task, then they're open to it again once the task is done and reassurance is what's needed.

The moment of highest stakes — picking a specific sectional — is exactly where Lovesac's copy goes silent.

The weak point in the real journey, stages 2 → 3

Their homepage does "Recognize" reasonably well with the animated scenario banner — but the names there don't survive into "Decide," where the customer actually needs them most. The redesign's naming work exists specifically to close that gap — carrying the same "is this for someone like me" feeling from the hero all the way into the product grid, instead of letting it evaporate right before the decision gets made.

Why This Is Strategy

A content strategy isn't "write everything warmly" or "write everything efficiently" — it's knowing, screen by screen, which job the copy is doing, and having the discipline to switch registers when the job changes. Every choice in this section — the idiom-borrowed voice, the scenario naming, the plain utility copy, even the honest gaps I haven't solved yet — comes from applying that one question consistently: what does this customer need to feel right now, and does this line of copy give them that, or get in the way of it?

That discipline is what "shaping brands with emotional intelligence" actually means in practice.

Scope

This case study covers a four-page concept, not a full site rebuild — homepage, sectional grid, support/contact, and the "what to expect" process page. The goal wasn't to polish one splashy hero; it was to prove the thinking holds up everywhere it touches, including the least glamorous screens. It's a starting point, and some pieces — like material-based color categorization — are flagged here as future research rather than finished answers.
