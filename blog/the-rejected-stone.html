Founder’s Notes

The rejected stone

Ask Read began with a simple thought: I wanted to build something good for someone.

Claudia Ochoa October 5, 2026 · 4 min read
01

Ask Read began with a simple thought: I wanted to build something good for someone.

There wasn’t some complicated strategy behind it. I saw something I thought could be useful—something that could make someone’s work a little easier, help them see things more clearly, and perhaps give them something they didn’t already have. So I started building it.

There is something incredibly hopeful about the beginning of an idea like that. Before the opinions. Before the questions. Before anyone tells you what it means, what it looks like, what you should be worried about, or why you shouldn’t be the person doing it. It is just you and the belief that maybe you can make something good.

What I wasn’t prepared for was how quickly the world can start chipping away at that spirit.

When you are trying to do something good for someone, you imagine the difficult part will be the work: learning what you don’t know, solving problems, getting things wrong and trying again, staying up too late because you finally figured something out and don’t want to lose the thought.

I can handle that kind of hard.

What is harder is having something that came from a good place begin to make you question whether creating it was a mistake. Lately, I have found myself wondering: Am I creating more anger than good?

That is a painful question to ask about something you built with care. And underneath it is another question that is much harder for me to ignore.

Would this experience be different if I were a white man?

I cannot answer that with certainty. I cannot tell you exactly which conversations would be different, which assumptions would disappear, which doors would open more easily, or which things I would never have to think about at all.

But I do know that I would not be dealing with everything I am dealing with now.

There is a particular exhaustion in knowing that fairness has never actually meant that everyone begins from the same place. We like to talk about good ideas as though they exist in a vacuum: if the idea is good enough, build it; if the work is good enough, people will see it; if your intentions are good, they will eventually become obvious.

But people do not encounter ideas without first encountering the person behind them. Sometimes you become painfully aware of that.

I don’t want that realization to make me bitter. I don’t want it to change the reason I started. And I especially don’t want other people’s assumptions to slowly become my own voice, telling me that perhaps I should make myself smaller, explain myself more, or walk away simply because continuing has become uncomfortable.

Maybe that is the part of building something nobody really talks about. Sometimes the thing being tested isn’t the idea. It’s your ability to protect the spirit you had when you first thought of it.

Ask Read is still, at its core, what it was when I began: an attempt to make something thoughtful for someone. I know where it came from. I know the care behind it. And despite everything, I still believe enough in the idea to keep building it.

But there is a question I haven’t been able to shake.

Let’s say I finish it. Let’s say I solve the problems, build the intelligence, get the details right, protect what needs protecting, and somehow turn this idea in my head into exactly what I believe it can be.

Then what?

Is there actually a way to get it to him?

That may be the hardest part of all of this to admit. I can build something for someone without ever knowing whether I will have the opportunity to place it in their hands.

There is no guarantee that Brock Purdy will ever see Ask Read. No guarantee that the right person will answer an email, take a meeting, forward a message, or believe for long enough that this is worth putting in front of him.

Sometimes I wonder what it means to put this much of yourself into something when the final distance between you and the person you built it for may be completely outside of your control.

Maybe I finish Ask Read and he never sees it.

I don’t know.

But I think I would rather live with having built something meaningful that never reached him than spend years wondering what might have happened if I had stopped because I was afraid it wouldn’t.

So for now, I build.

And when it is finished, I suppose I will find out whether the hardest part was building Ask Read—or finding a way for Ask Read to find him.
